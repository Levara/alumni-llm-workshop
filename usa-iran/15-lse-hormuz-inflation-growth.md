# Disruption in the Strait of Hormuz: A Global Inflation, Shipping, and Growth Story

**Source:** LSE Business Review
**Author:** Mohsen Khezri, Visiting Senior Fellow at LSE
**URL:** https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/2026/03/12/disruption-in-the-strait-of-hormuz-is-a-global-inflation-shipping-and-growth-story/
**Date:** March 12, 2026

## Overview

The article examines how disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz represent far more than a regional oil issue -- they constitute a global inflation and growth threat. Khezri characterizes the strait as an "economic clock of war," where the duration of closure directly impacts economic sustainability.

## Key Facts and Figures

### Global Oil Dependency

- Approximately 20 million barrels per day of oil transit the strait (roughly 20-25% of global consumption)
- About 19% of global liquified natural gas trade depends on Hormuz
- 80-84% of crude flows are directed to Asian markets

### Pipeline Alternatives

- Only 3.5-5.5 million barrels daily can be rerouted through Saudi and Emirati pipelines
- A complete closure creates a net shortfall of 14.5-16.5 million barrels daily

### Emergency Stockpiles

- Global public emergency reserves: 1.2+ billion barrels
- Industry stocks under government obligation: approximately 600 million barrels
- IEA announced a 400-million-barrel coordinated release on March 11, 2026 -- "the largest in its history"

### Regional Resilience Varies

- Japan: reserves covering approximately 254 days of consumption
- South Korea: approximately 208 days of coverage
- China: approximately 900 million barrels (approximately three months of imports)
- India: approximately 20-25 days of current inventory

## Critical Analysis

Khezri argues that while strategic reserves prevent immediate catastrophic collapse, they cannot sustain prolonged disruption. The economically tolerable window for closure spans approximately one to three months rather than six months.

### Three Disruption Phases

1. **Short-term:** Strategic releases limit price spikes
2. **Medium-duration:** Prices rise as inventories deplete and uncertainty persists
3. **Long-term:** Broader inflation emerges through diesel shortages, shipping insurance costs, and transport expenses

## Strategic Implications

The author contends that disruptions create cumulative economic danger. Initial oil shocks evolve into broader inflation and growth crises as disruption extends. This dynamic creates inherent time pressure on conflict duration, as vulnerable importers face intensifying political and macroeconomic costs before physical shortages occur.

The analysis suggests the Strait's commercial viability -- not military outcomes alone -- may ultimately determine the conflict's economic sustainability.
