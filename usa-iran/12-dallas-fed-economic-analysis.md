# Strait of Hormuz Closure: Global Economic Impact Analysis

**Source:** Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas
**Authors:** Lutz Kilian, Michael Plante, Alexander W. Richter
**URL:** https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0320
**Date:** March 20, 2026

## Overview

On February 28, 2026, military conflict between Iran, the United States, and Israel prompted closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint for global oil exports. Researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas quantified potential economic consequences across various scenarios.

## Scale of Disruption

This geopolitical event is unprecedented in magnitude. The closure removes approximately 20 percent of global oil supplies from markets -- roughly three to five times larger than previous disruptions. For context:

- The 1973 and 1990 oil crises removed only 6 percent of global supplies
- The 1979-1980 disruptions affected 4 percent

The Persian Gulf supplies about 80 percent of its production to Asian markets, making alternative sourcing difficult for importers facing restricted access.

## Economic Projections

According to the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas model:

### One-Quarter Closure Scenario

- Oil prices: $98 WTI per barrel in Q2 2026, declining to $68 in Q3
- Global GDP growth impact: -2.9 percentage points (annualized) in Q2; recovery of +2.2 points in Q3
- Year-end GDP remains 0.2 percent below baseline

### Extended Closures

- Two-quarter closure: Oil reaches $115 per barrel by Q3; annual GDP growth declines 0.3 percentage points
- Three-quarter closure: Oil peaks at $132 per barrel; annual GDP growth falls 1.3 percentage points

## Mitigation Strategies

Potential approaches to reduce supply shortfall include:

- Saudi Arabia redirecting 4 million barrels daily via Red Sea pipelines (roughly one-fifth of shortfall)
- Diplomatic negotiations allowing selective tanker passage to specific nations
- Resuming operations in hostile conditions similar to 1980s "Tanker Wars"

## Key Finding

"Reducing the shortfall from 20 percent to 10 percent would lessen the effect on quarterly global real GDP growth from -2.9 percentage points to -1.6 percentage points."
