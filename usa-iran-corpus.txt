# The 2026 Iran War: Comprehensive Overview

**Source:** Britannica
**URL:** https://www.britannica.com/event/2026-Iran-War

## Definition and Initiation

The 2026 Iran War represents a major conflict initiated by the United States and Israel on February 28, 2026. "Operation Epic Fury" was the U.S. code name for its joint military operations with Israel against Iran. This confrontation fundamentally altered Middle Eastern dynamics and had significant global repercussions.

## Background and Causes

### Historical Context

Tensions between America and Iran trace back decades. The U.S. involvement in the 1953 coup d'etat that propped up the unpopular Pahlavi monarchy created lasting grievances. The 1979 Islamic Revolution and subsequent hostage crisis deepened mutual distrust.

### Immediate Precipitating Factors

Several developments weakened Iran's regional position:

- The 2024 Israel-Hamas War diminished Iranian influence significantly
- Israel's September-November 2024 campaign against Hezbollah in Lebanon severed a crucial Iranian alliance
- The December 2024 overthrow of Syrian President Bashar al-Assad removed a pro-Iran leader
- A June 2025 conflict exposed vulnerabilities in Iran's air defenses and damaged military infrastructure
- Economic collapse followed the 12-day War, with Iran's currency entering a free fall
- Widespread protests erupted in December 2025, met with brutal security crackdowns

### Nuclear and Military Concerns

The U.S. and Israel focused on Iran's nuclear program, ballistic missiles, and military reach across the region. Failed diplomatic negotiations after the 2015 nuclear agreement (JCPOA) eliminated peaceful resolution pathways.

## Timeline of Major Events

**February 28, 2026:** The campaign commenced with nearly 900 strikes in 12 hours. Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and dozens of other top Iranian officials were killed. A missile strike on a girls' school near Bandar Abbas resulted in approximately 170 civilian deaths.

**March 2, 2026:** Hezbollah launched missiles and drones into Israel, escalating the Lebanon component of the conflict.

**March 3, 2026:** Israeli airstrikes targeted Iran's Assembly of Experts to prevent it from selecting a new supreme leader.

**March 5, 2026:** President Trump indicated he wanted involvement in choosing Khamenei's successor.

**March 17, 2026:** Ali Larijani, described as a pragmatic senior official and key JCPOA architect, was killed.

## Parties Involved

**Primary Combatants:**
- United States
- Israel
- Iran

**Secondary Participants:**
- Hezbollah (Lebanon)
- Various Middle Eastern nations hosting U.S. military installations
- NATO forces (Turkey)

**Regional Powers Affected:**
Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Iraq, Oman, and Jordan experienced attacks on embassies and military facilities.

## Military Operations and Tactics

The initial U.S.-Israeli operation targeted Iranian missiles and air defenses, other military infrastructure, and Iranian leadership. Iran responded with hundreds of retaliatory missiles and thousands of drones across the region, employing what scholar Robert A. Pape described as "horizontal escalation" -- widening the conflict beyond pure military dimensions into political and economic realms.

## Casualties and Displacement

The conflict produced devastating human costs:

- More than 2,000 people dead in Iran, Lebanon, and Israel
- Hundreds of thousands of people displaced in Lebanon
- At least 30,000 killed during January 2026 security crackdowns preceding the war

## Global Consequences

### Transportation Disruption

Flights in and out of the Middle East came to a near-complete stop, stranding residents and travelers. Dubai International Airport sustained drone strike damage, temporarily halting all flights.

### Energy and Trade

Shipping lines rerouted to avoid the Strait of Hormuz and Red Sea. Oil disruptions triggered fuel shortages and rationing in parts of Asia and a sharp increase in price in the global oil market.

### Diplomatic Responses

Reactions varied significantly. Some nations questioned the legality of initial strikes. Spain, a NATO member, refused to provide air bases to the U.S., prompting trade threats. Russia condemned the attacks but showed reluctance to intervene directly.

## Ongoing Developments

As of late March 2026, diplomatic efforts were accelerating. Pakistan's prime minister offered to facilitate negotiations. Trump expressed openness to negotiated settlements, though Iran's new supreme leader (Mojtaba Khamenei, Ali's son) inherited a devastated nation grappling with both external military pressure and internal instability.

# After the Strike: The Danger of War in Iran

**Source:** Brookings Institution
**URL:** https://www.brookings.edu/articles/after-the-strike-the-danger-of-war-in-iran/
**Date:** February/March 2026

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel launched a joint military operation against Iran that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. Brookings Institution experts analyzed the conflict's implications for Iran, the region, and the world.

## Iran's Situation

Suzanne Maloney compared Khamenei's death to the 1979 Shah's departure, noting that while both were momentous, hope for genuine change may be premature. The regime's embedded institutions retain significant advantages over challengers. Iran is deploying escalating strikes on neighbors' infrastructure to create diplomatic pressure.

Itamar Rabinovich observed the regime's radical response, attacking multiple Arab neighbors including Oman and targeting Cyprus. He suggested the conflict could last weeks or months, and questioned whether Trump possesses the patience for a prolonged campaign.

Vanda Felbab-Brown warned about the limits of targeting leadership alone. She emphasized the regime's vast structure -- religious authority, military layers, and economic control -- won't simply disappear despite decapitation strikes. Future instability could produce worse outcomes than the status quo.

## Military Dimensions

Mara Karlin described the operation as historic, employing sophisticated conventional capabilities paired with intelligence and strategic planning. She noted Iranian responses spanned "low to high end of the continuum of conflict," with drones proving more effective than missiles at evading air defenses.

Michael E. O'Hanlon and Caitlin Talmadge examined airpower's track record in regime change attempts, citing Kosovo, Afghanistan, Iraq, and Libya. They concluded the historical evidence suggests a "very difficult and slow" path ahead.

## Regional Consequences

### Turkey's Position

Asli Aydintasbas warned the conflict sharpens Turkey-Israel enmity, pushing them toward collision. Turkey fears Israeli regional primacy more than Iranian weakness. Kemal Kirisci highlighted Turkey's refugee concerns, noting previous Syrian experience left 2.3 million displaced persons, straining resources and politics.

### Europe's Dilemma

Constanze Stelzenmuller described European governments as uncomfortable, relieved at the regime's overthrow but apprehensive about destabilization. The E-3 (France, Germany, UK) declared willingness to conduct "defensive action" against Iranian missile capabilities.

### Russia's Response

Pavel K. Baev noted Putin's shock and concern, particularly given his obsession with personal safety following Gadhafi's death. Steven Pifer observed Moscow's response would remain rhetorical -- condemning actions while taking no meaningful steps.

### China and Taiwan

Ryan Hass explained China views Iran as a mid-tier partner and will focus on restoring oil flows. Taiwan hopes for rapid American mission completion to refocus deterrence on China.

### India's Stakes

Tanvi Madan emphasized India's significant interests -- 9 million citizens in the region, nearly half its oil imports, and crucial remittances. New Delhi seeks cessation of hostilities and de-escalation.

## International Law and Southeast Asia

Lynn Kuok warned the strikes normalize unilateral force as statecraft, threatening the UN Charter's prohibition on force. She noted the Strait of Hormuz disruption risks and the "Ghost Fleet" of dark tankers off Malaysia's coast.

## Domestic U.S. Implications

William A. Galston criticized the attack as occurring "without congressional authorization," breaching constitutional checks and balances. He noted previous presidents sought congressional backing before major military ventures.

Scott R. Anderson explained the executive branch maintains broad authority over military force without congressional approval, creating problematic precedents for regime change operations.

## Economic Factors

Samantha Gross observed oil markets responded with surprising restraint -- only 4.5% increases despite 20 percent of global oil supplies flowing through the Strait of Hormuz. She noted limited circumvention capacity exists.

## Uncertain Outcomes

Sharan Grewal predicted a "Venezuela scenario" remained most likely -- Trump striking deals with successor regimes rather than pursuing prolonged warfare. Steven Heydemann warned regime collapse could produce worse alternatives, sustained instability, or renewed authoritarianism.

Stephanie T. Williams expressed concern about "flying blind," noting no clear post-war planning exists and comparing risks to Iraq and Libya interventions.

Amnesty International released findings from an investigation into a deadly US military strike on Shajareh Tayyebeh Elementary School in Minab, Iran on February 28, 2026. The attack killed 168 people, including over 100 children.

The organization's Evidence Lab analyzed satellite imagery, videos, photographs, and witness testimonies. Their conclusion: the school building was directly struck by precision-guided weapons, marking a serious breach of international humanitarian law.

Evidence of violations included:

- The school had been physically separated from an adjacent IRGC military compound since at least 2016, with distinct walls and entrances
- US forces appeared to rely on outdated intelligence, failing to verify the building's civilian status
- The strike employed guided munitions, indicating intentional targeting rather than indiscriminate fire

## International Humanitarian Law Concerns

Amnesty International emphasized that parties to conflicts must "take all feasible precautions to avoid civilian harm" including verifying targets are military objectives.

The organization noted two possible scenarios both constituting violations:

1. **Gross negligence:** Failing to identify a school building before striking it
2. **Reckless indifference:** Knowing about the school's proximity and attacking without adequate precautions

## Accountability Demands

Amnesty called for:

- Independent, transparent investigation by US authorities
- Public disclosure of findings
- Prosecution where sufficient evidence exists
- Full reparations for victims and families

## Additional Concerns

The report documented Iranian authorities exploiting bereaved families and traumatized survivors for propaganda purposes, forcing participation in mass funerals contrary to families' religious traditions and filming distressed children at school ruins.

Erika Guevara-Rosas stated the attack represents "the catastrophic and entirely predictable price civilians are paying during this armed conflict."

# The U.S. War on Iran: New and Lingering Nuclear Risks

**Source:** Arms Control Association
**URL:** https://www.armscontrol.org/issue-briefs/2026-03/us-war-iran-new-and-lingering-nuclear-risks
**Author:** Kelsey Davenport
**Date:** March 10, 2026

## Overview

This issue brief examines nuclear proliferation and security risks stemming from U.S. and Israeli military strikes on Iran beginning February 28, 2026. While military action may temporarily degrade Iran's nuclear infrastructure, it cannot eliminate Tehran's capacity to eventually develop nuclear weapons, and the ongoing conflict creates additional safety hazards.

## Iran's 60% Enriched Uranium Stockpile

Prior to June 2025 strikes, Iran had produced approximately 440 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60% U-235 -- material requiring only further enrichment to reach weapons-grade levels. The vast majority existed in UF6 gas form stored in small canisters.

Following June 2025 attacks, the IAEA assessed that roughly 200 kilograms of 60% material remains underground at Esfahan, sufficient for approximately five nuclear warheads if enriched to 90% weapons-grade. Additional quantities may remain at Natanz and Fordow, though exact locations and quantities remain uncertain due to Iran's pre-strike dispersal efforts.

## Proposed U.S. Seizure Operation

President Trump has suggested deploying special forces to physically seize Iran's enriched uranium stockpile. Such an operation presents significant risks:

- **Chemical hazards:** UF6 gas is toxic and requires specialized protective equipment
- **Radiological danger:** 60% enriched uranium can sustain fission reactions, complicating transportation
- **Operational complexity:** Multiple sites may require recovery, extending troop exposure
- **Logistical burden:** Deploying the Mobile Uranium Facility would require extensive support infrastructure

Some Republican lawmakers have already opposed potential ground operations.

## Limitations of Material Removal

Even complete removal of 60% uranium would not eliminate proliferation risks. Iran would retain:

- Lower-enriched uranium (5-20%) stockpiles at multiple locations
- Centrifuge production capacity and technical expertise
- Knowledge to convert materials and produce weapons components

The timeline for Iranian nuclear weapons development remains uncertain given infrastructure damage and unknown variables regarding Iranian prioritization of speed versus secrecy.

## Centrifuge Production Uncertainties

The IAEA lost tracking of Iran's centrifuge production in 2021. Between 2021 and June 2025, Iran possessed capacity to manufacture more centrifuges than documented as installed. Some machines may exist at undeclared facilities, and Iran retains knowledge to produce additional centrifuges. Complete seizure of all centrifuges remains infeasible.

## Long-Term Implications

A U.S. ground seizure operation could complicate future IAEA verification efforts and accountability measures. Documentation of removed materials before operations could mitigate these complications, though hostile environments present unforeseen challenges.

Additionally, territorial violation through ground incursions may strengthen arguments within Iran for nuclear weapons development as deterrence.

## Nuclear Facility Targeting in Operation Epic Fury

Strikes occurring March 2 and beyond targeted:

- **Natanz:** Struck to prevent facility access
- **Min Zadei:** Israeli claims this northeastern Tehran site relates to nuclear weaponization (IAEA disputes structured weapons program evidence)
- **Associated facilities:** Sites near the Atomic Energy Organization of Iran and SPND
- **Pickaxe facility:** Reportedly a centrifuge assembly site under construction; no confirmed strikes

Nuclear facilities were not targeted in initial February 28 strikes.

## Reactor Safety Risks

**Bushehr Power Reactor:** Operated by Russia's Rosatom at full capacity. The facility director warned that strikes would constitute "a regional-scale disaster," though deliberate targeting remains unlikely.

**Research Reactors:** Iran operates the Tehran Research Reactor and MNSR reactor at Esfahan.

Accidental strikes on reactors or supporting infrastructure could cause meltdowns and radioactive release. Iran's non-membership in the Convention on Nuclear Safety raises questions about safety protocols.

## Regional Nuclear Implications

Iranian officials reportedly threatened targeting Israel's Dimona nuclear weapons facility if regime change is pursued. Past studies indicate significant radiological consequences from such strikes.

The UAE operates four reactors at the Barakah nuclear power plant. Accidental strikes or infrastructure damage could compromise safe operations with regional consequences.

## Nuclear Terrorism and Material Diversion Risks

Currently, non-state actors face substantial barriers to weaponizing enriched uranium. However, governmental collapse or significant internal instability could increase risks of material theft or diversion to undeclared locations. Additionally, Iranian nuclear scientists might consider selling expertise to state or non-state nuclear weapons aspirants.

# Operation Epic Fury and the Remnants of Iran's Nuclear Program

**Source:** Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS)
**Authors:** Joseph Rodgers and Bailey Schiff
**URL:** https://www.csis.org/analysis/operation-epic-fury-and-remnants-irans-nuclear-program
**Date:** February 28, 2026

## Overview

The United States and Israel launched coordinated military operations against Iran on February 28, 2026. The U.S. operation was called Operation Epic Fury, while Israel designated theirs Operation Roaring Lion. President Trump announced the strikes via TruthSocial at 2:00 AM EST, stating "the hour of your freedom is at hand" in a message directed at Iranians.

## Military Objectives

According to Trump's announcement, Operation Epic Fury aimed to accomplish four military goals:

1. Preventing Iran from acquiring nuclear weapons
2. Destroying missile arsenals and production facilities
3. Degrading proxy networks
4. Eliminating Iran's naval forces

Trump explained the rationale, declaring that Iran "rejected every opportunity to renounce their nuclear ambitions" and that the regime was "attempting to rebuild their nuclear program" with missiles threatening allies and potentially American territory.

The strikes differed significantly from Operation Midnight Hammer (June 2025), which targeted only nuclear facilities. The February 28 operations struck leadership positions, military installations, missile production centers, and remnants of the nuclear program, suggesting a broader objective of systematic degradation of Iran's government.

## Nuclear Targets

Operation Midnight Hammer had already dealt substantial damage to Iran's nuclear infrastructure, destroying enrichment facilities at Fordow and Natanz, plus metallurgy operations at Isfahan. Iran had not substantially rehabilitated these sites since June 2025.

The current strikes shifted focus toward peripheral nuclear capabilities. Initial reports indicated targets included administrative centers and dual-use research facilities. Unconfirmed reports suggested strikes on the Iran Atomic Energy Agency headquarters in Tehran and the explosive testing facility at Parchin, with additional strikes reported at the Isfahan nuclear complex.

The Bushehr reactor -- operated with assistance from Russia's Rosatom corporation -- presented escalation risks. Russia evacuated approximately 100 personnel, though some remained to maintain reactor operations. Explosions were reported in Bushehr's port area, but no confirmed direct strikes on the reactor occurred.

## Negotiation Breakdown

Three rounds of negotiations occurred throughout February, ultimately failing to reach agreement. Core disagreement centered on whether Iran's ballistic missile programs and proxy networks should be included, and whether Iran could continue uranium enrichment.

**First Round (February 6, Muscat, Oman):**
U.S. Special Envoy Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi. Araghchi insisted talks address "solely the nuclear issue," while Trump maintained any agreement must cover nuclear weapons, missiles, and related concerns. Mohammad Eslami, heading Iran's Atomic Energy Organization, proposed diluting Iran's 400-kilogram stockpile of 60 percent enriched uranium and restoring International Atomic Energy Agency access in exchange for sanctions relief.

**Second Round (February 17, Geneva):**
Both sides agreed to draft proposals, with the U.S. setting a month-end deadline for Iran's final proposal. Araghchi claimed the U.S. had not demanded "zero enrichment," while Witkoff countered that Iran had crossed Trump's red line on enrichment requirements.

**Third Round (February 26, Geneva):**
Oman claimed Iran agreed never to stockpile enriched uranium, calling it "a major breakthrough." However, interpretations diverged significantly. Iranian representatives proposed only diluting highly enriched uranium domestically, whereas American officials insisted enriched uranium be transferred abroad. Trump stated his position: "I say no enrichment. Not 20 percent, 30 percent," while Araghchi emphasized Iran's right to "peaceful nuclear energy, including enrichment."

## Iranian Retaliation

Iran launched Operation True Promise 4, striking Israel and multiple U.S. military targets across the Middle East within hours. Explosions were reported in the UAE, Bahrain, Qatar, Dubai, and Saudi Arabia.

This Iranian response triggered significant regional developments. Saudi Arabia declared it would "take all necessary measures to defend its security and protect its territory," signaling willingness to respond directly. The UAE similarly indicated readiness to retaliate, citing civilian casualties from Iranian strikes.

Internet connectivity in Iran dropped by at least 46 percent, suggesting extensive cyberattacks were underway. Iran has historically employed limited cyberoperations to contain escalation; however, analysts noted little room for de-escalation given the systematic nature of current strikes.

## Proliferation Concerns

The analysis concluded that kinetic strikes introduced new proliferation risks despite reducing short-term proliferation dangers. Iran still possessed 400 kilograms of 60 percent enriched uranium, with its exact location unknown. Dispersal of Iranian nuclear and missile scientists during conflict could pose proliferation risks to non-state actors or hostile nations. Potential collapse of Iran's Atomic Energy Organization could scatter specialized expertise with serious consequences.

## Conclusion

The authors characterized Operations Epic Fury and Roaring Lion as likely initiating prolonged conflict. The U.S.-Israel coalition moved beyond targeting proliferation capabilities toward attempting to destroy the Iranian government entirely, spreading conflict across multiple Middle Eastern countries within the initial hours.

# The Iran War is Causing Energy Chaos in Asia

**Source:** Council on Foreign Relations (CFR)
**Authors:** Joshua Kurlantzick and Annabel Richter
**URL:** https://www.cfr.org/articles/the-iran-war-is-causing-energy-chaos-in-asia
**Date:** March 18, 2026

## Overview

Following U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran, the country's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz has created severe energy supply disruptions across Asia, pushing the region toward potential economic collapse and social unrest.

## Current Crisis Dimensions

Oil futures closed at $95 per barrel as major Gulf oil and gas facilities suffered damage. Strikes targeted:

- Ras Tanura refinery (Saudi Arabia)
- Ras Laffan gas processing base (Qatar)
- Ruwais refinery complex (UAE)

Combined with Iran's Strait of Hormuz blockade, Gulf oil production has dropped by 10 million barrels daily compared to March 2025. Oil flow through the Strait has plummeted to "less than 10 percent of pre-war levels."

## Asia's Vulnerability

In 2024, "84% of the oil and 83% of the liquified natural gas (LNG)" transiting the Strait was destined for Asia. Major economies -- China, Japan, Singapore, Taiwan, South Korea, India, and Thailand -- depend almost entirely on foreign oil supplies.

## Government Responses

Asian nations have implemented aggressive conservation measures:

- **Philippines:** Four-day workweeks; computers switched off during lunch; air conditioning set to minimum 24C (75F)
- **Vietnam:** Work-from-home encouragement
- **Thailand:** Officials urged to use stairs instead of elevators
- **Multiple nations:** Fuel rationing; strategic reserve releases

## Economic Damage

Factories operate part-time or have closed entirely. Tourism -- critical for Thailand, Vietnam, and Singapore -- has collapsed. Thailand tourist arrivals fell approximately 9 percent year-on-year in early March, with hotels reporting occupancy rates as low as 10 percent.

## Subsidy Crises

Governments are absorbing fuel price increases, but unsustainably:

- **Indonesia:** Subsidies threaten budget deficits exceeding the legal 3 percent GDP cap
- **India:** "The scale of demand in India, the world's most populous nation, limits how long it can cap prices"

## Political Risk

Historical precedent warns of danger. Indonesia's 1998 uprising -- partly triggered by fuel price spikes during financial crisis -- ultimately toppled the Suharto regime. Current protests have already turned violent in some nations. Bangladesh has closed universities and deployed military personnel to guard oil depots.

## Outlook

The authors warn: "If it lasts through the summer, it could have calamitous effects on Asian growth and political stability." The war, merely twenty days old at publication, threatens both economic devastation and unprecedented regional instability.

# Iran War's Impact on Gas Markets in Europe, Asia Will Last for Years

**Source:** Foreign Policy
**Author:** Keith Johnson
**URL:** https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/03/23/iran-war-natural-gas-lng-prices-markets-impact-qatar-asia/
**Date:** March 23, 2026

## Overview

The ongoing conflict between the United States, Israel, and Iran is creating severe disruptions to global liquefied natural gas (LNG) markets, with effects expected to persist long after hostilities cease.

## Current Situation

President Trump announced "productive conversations" with Iran via Truth Social, claiming his envoy Steve Witkoff and son-in-law Jared Kushner had engaged with senior Iranian officials. However, Iran's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf denied any such contacts, suggesting Trump's message aimed to manipulate markets.

Despite diplomatic claims, Israel continued major strikes on Tehran immediately after Trump's announcement. Oil prices fell and stock markets rose temporarily following the announcement.

## LNG Supply Crisis

Approximately one-fifth of the world's LNG normally transits through the Strait of Hormuz, which remains effectively closed except for vessels Iran permits. Qatar and the UAE supply roughly 90 percent of Persian Gulf gas to Asian markets.

Key impacts:

- Gas prices in Asia have doubled since hostilities began
- The final pre-war LNG tankers are now arriving; no additional shipments are en route
- Countries including China, Japan, Taiwan, Pakistan, and Bangladesh face cascading energy impacts

Anne-Sophie Corbeau from Columbia University's Center on Global Energy Policy noted: "It's a big impact on China, Japan, Taiwan, Pakistan..." with effects spanning "power generation and industry."

## Qatar's Damage

Qatar shut down all LNG production at its Ras Laffan facility following Iranian attacks that severely damaged two of fourteen liquefaction units. QatarEnergy reports two damaged trains -- representing 17 percent of Qatar's production -- will require three to five years to repair, causing $20 billion in annual revenue losses.

Restarting undamaged units will take four to six weeks minimum if everything proceeds well. Corbeau stated: "These trains need to be restarted sequentially, one or two at a time, and they need to restart them very slowly."

## Long-Term Outlook

Even in best-case scenarios, global LNG markets won't return to pre-war supply levels until late 2027. New production from Australia, Canada, and the United States won't compensate for lost Persian Gulf supplies.

Europe faces particular concern, having endured energy trauma since Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. The continent depends on summer LNG imports to refill storage for winter resilience against Russian attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure.

Corbeau warned: "2026 is going to be at the same level as last year, and we may not even see the big relief in 2027."

## Market Responses

Goldman Sachs predicts oil prices will remain in triple digits near-term. Trump offered sanctions relief to Russia and Iran to increase oil supplies, enabling previously stranded crude to reach markets. Iran particularly benefits, earning additional billions by selling oil at market prices to Asian buyers, especially China.

# Threat Brief: March 2026 Escalation of Cyber Risk Related to Iran

**Source:** Unit 42 / Palo Alto Networks
**URL:** https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/iranian-cyberattacks-2026/
**Date:** March 2026

## Executive Summary

Following Operation Epic Fury (U.S.) and Operation Roaring Lion (Israel) launched February 28, 2026, Iran initiated a multi-vector retaliatory cyber campaign. Unit 42 observed escalated cyberattacks from activist groups, though nation-state operations face constraints due to Iran's severely degraded internet connectivity -- dropping to 1-4% availability.

The loss of connectivity and command structure degradation will "likely hinder the ability of state-aligned threat actors to coordinate and execute sophisticated cyberattacks in the near-term." State-aligned units may operate in isolation, while dispersed operators could conduct low-to-medium sophistication disruptions like DDoS attacks and data exfiltration campaigns.

## Current Threat Activity

### Malicious Mobile Campaign

Unit 42 identified an active phishing campaign distributing a malicious Android package impersonating the Israeli Home Front Command RedAlert application, delivering mobile surveillance and data-exfiltrating malware.

### Hacktivist Groups

Approximately 60 individual hacktivist groups became active as of March 2, 2026, including:

**Handala Hack** (MOIS-affiliated): The most prominent Iranian persona, claiming responsibility for compromising Israeli energy companies, Jordan's fuel systems, and Israeli healthcare networks to create domestic pressure.

**APT Iran**: A pro-Iranian collective conducting hack-and-leak operations against Jordan's critical infrastructure.

**Cyber Islamic Resistance**: An umbrella collective coordinating synchronized DDoS attacks, data-wiping operations, and website defacements against Israeli and Western infrastructure.

- Article I of the Constitution grants Congress sole war declaration authority
- The ACLU has maintained this position consistently across administrations for over 50 years

## Constitutional Framework

The Constitution deliberately distributes war powers to prevent concentrated authority. As one expert notes: "The Constitution gives Congress the sole authority to declare war, except in truly exceptional circumstances" like defense against actual attack or imminent danger.

The founders specifically rejected concentrating war-making power in a single individual, having witnessed European monarchs' unchecked military decisions.

## Trump's Justification

Trump has justified the attacks by arguing that despite holding talks with Iran, he believed Tehran was planning to strike first -- thus invoking the "self-defense" justification. The ACLU argues this justification is insufficient for sustained military operations without congressional approval.

## Congressional Remedies

### War Powers Resolution

Congress can invoke this 1973 law requiring the president to terminate unauthorized force if both chambers vote to do so, or after 60 days of undeclared military action. However, the 60-day clock is contested -- administrations have historically argued it doesn't apply to their particular operations.

### Appropriations Authority

Congress controls military funding and can refuse supplemental appropriations bills for unauthorized wars, forcing the administration to seek additional resources through democratic votes. This "power of the purse" represents Congress's most potent constitutional tool.

## Historical Context

The article documents how war declarations historically enabled domestic civil liberties violations, including:

- Espionage Act enforcement during WWI (jailing critics of the war)
- Japanese American incarceration during WWII
- Post-9/11 surveillance and detention programs (Guantanamo, warrantless wiretapping)

The ACLU argues that the lack of formal congressional authorization makes the current military action constitutionally suspect and sets dangerous precedents for executive overreach.
